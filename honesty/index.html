Doodle Honesty

How do you find out whether someone is being straight with you? The research has a clear answer, and it is not the one anybody wants: you cannot tell by looking. What you can do is check. This page is about the difference.

1.Reading people, and why it fails

Humans detect lies 54% of the time. A coin gets 50%. Worse, if you act on that ability in a group where most people are honest, almost everyone you accuse is innocent — and that part is just arithmetic.

measured — the 54% figure pools 206 studies and 24,483 judges (Bond & DePaulo, 2006). The absence of reliable behavioural cues is a separate meta-analysis (DePaulo et al., 2003). The polygraph's unsuitability for screening is the conclusion of a US National Research Council review. The base-rate panel is not a study at all — it is Bayes' theorem, and it cannot be argued with.

the papers behind this simulation — search these in your library: 2003 DePaulo, Lindsay, Malone, Muhlenbruck, Charlton, Cooper - Cues to Deception 2003 National Research Council - The Polygraph and Lie Detection 2006 Bond, DePaulo - Accuracy of Deception Judgments 2006 Global Deception Research Team - A World of Lies 2006 Hartwig, Granhag, Strömwall, Kronkvist - Strategic Use of Evidence During Police Interviews 2014 Levine - Truth-Default Theory (TDT): A Theory of Human Deception and Deception Detection
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
base rate
How common the thing is before you look. If 5 people in 100 are lying, that 5% is the base rate — and it dominates everything, because it decides how many honest people are available to be misjudged.
sensitivity
Of the people who ARE lying, what fraction do you catch? Humans: 47%. We miss most lies.
specificity
Of the people who are being honest, what fraction do you correctly leave alone? Humans: 61%. Which means we wrongly suspect nearly 4 honest people in 10.
precision
The number that actually matters when you accuse someone: of everyone you flag, what fraction really lied? At realistic human accuracy and a low base rate it is around 6%. Nineteen of every twenty accusations land on someone innocent.
truth-default
Levine's finding that people mostly assume they are being told the truth, and only leave that default when something forces them to. It looks like gullibility. It is closer to efficiency: most statements really are true, and checking everything would cost more than it saves.
strategic use of evidence
An interview technique with actual support: don't reveal what you already know. Let the account be given freely first, then compare it with the evidence you were holding. Liars unwittingly contradict facts they didn't know you had; truth-tellers don't.
$$ P(\text{lying} \mid \text{you accused}) = \frac{b \cdot s}{b \cdot s + (1-b)(1-t)} $$

b = base rate of lying, s = sensitivity, t = specificity. With the measured human values (s = 0.47, t = 0.61) and 5% of a group lying, this comes out at about 6% — so roughly 94 of every 100 accusations are wrong. The panel computes it live for any values you choose.

The instinct that you can read a person is one of the most confidently held beliefs there is, and one of the least supported. Bond and DePaulo pooled 206 studies covering 24,483 people making lie-or-truth judgements. Average accuracy: 54%. Coin-flipping gets 50%. Training, experience and professional confidence barely move it — police officers and judges score about the same as students, while being considerably more certain.

Break the number down and it gets more interesting. We correctly flag only 47% of lies, but correctly clear 61% of truths. We are biased toward belief. Timothy Levine argues this is not a bug: most of what people tell us is true, so defaulting to trust is efficient, and the occasional lie that slips through costs less than checking everything would. Our lie detector is bad because it mostly does not need to be good.

The cues everyone "knows" do not survive measurement. DePaulo and colleagues meta-analysed the behavioural signals — gaze, fidgeting, hesitation, story detail — and found them faint to non-existent. Avoiding eye contact is believed to indicate lying in nearly every country surveyed, and it does not work anywhere. Nervousness indicates nervousness; an honest person who realises they are suspected becomes more nervous, not less. That is why the two figures in the first panel are drawn with the same random seed. There is no visible difference to draw.

Now the panel that matters, because it is arithmetic rather than psychology. Suppose you act on your judgement in a group where 5% are lying. Even with the measured human hit rates, you end up accusing about 39 people in every 100 — and only about 2 of them actually lied. Your accusation is right roughly 6% of the time. Switch to a fictional detector that is 90% accurate in both directions and it is still wrong about two-thirds of the time at that base rate. This is exactly the mathematics of screening for a rare disease, and it does not care how certain you feel.

The practical conclusion is not "look harder", it is "stop looking". Every method with real support has the same shape: it compares the claim against something outside the person. Check what can be checked — dates, records, who else was present, what was written down at the time. Ask questions that are cheap to answer if you lived it and expensive to answer if you are constructing it. Hold back what you already know and see whether the account collides with it; that last one, strategic use of evidence, is the best-supported interview technique in the literature.

And notice what all of those have in common: none of them require you to decide whether someone is a liar. They require you to find out what is true. Those are different jobs, and only the second one is achievable. Someone can be entirely sincere and still wrong; someone can be evasive because they are constrained rather than dishonest. If your conclusion rests on how a person seemed, you do not yet have a conclusion — you have an impression, and the evidence says it is worth about four percentage points above a coin.
