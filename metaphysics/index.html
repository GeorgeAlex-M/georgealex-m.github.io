Doodle Metaphysics

The questions that sit underneath the physics: what exists, what makes a thing that thing, what a cause is, whether the future is already there. Every section carries an honest label saying whether measured evidence reaches the question at all — because for most of them, it does not.

“The world is the totality of facts, not of things.” * Ludwig Wittgenstein

★How to read this page

The rest of this site labels claims by how well they are measured. Metaphysics needs a different scale, because most of its questions cannot be measured even in principle — and pretending otherwise would be the exact failure this page exists to avoid.

Four labels are used here, and the difference between them matters more than any individual argument:

constrained by measured physics — experiment genuinely bears on the question. You cannot hold just any position; some are ruled out by data. Rare, and the most interesting case.

conceptual analysis — argument alone. No experiment could settle it, even with unlimited budget, because the disagreement is not about what happens but about what the words pick out.

live disagreement — competent, informed people hold opposing views today, and their reasons are stateable. Not the same as "anyone's guess".

verbal dispute — the argument dissolves once the terms are pinned down. Both sides were right about the facts and disagreeing about a definition. Philosophy's most useful and least celebrated result.

A section can start in one category and end in another — and when a piece of physics does real work, it will be marked exactly where the physics stops and the interpretation begins. That line is the whole point.

1.Is the future already there?

Two people pass on a pavement. One is walking. Their "now", carried out to Andromeda, differs by three days. That is measured physics — and then the argument starts.

constrained by measured physics — the relativity of simultaneity is not an interpretation. It follows from the measured constancy of the speed of light, and it is confirmed daily by particle accelerators and GPS. What it implies about whether the future exists is a separate question, and this section marks precisely where the physics stops and the philosophy begins.

doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
simultaneity
Which distant events count as happening "at the same time as" this one. It feels like a fact about the universe. It is not: it depends on how fast you are moving, and there is no way to settle it that all observers would accept.
plane of "now"
The set of all events an observer calls simultaneous with themselves. Draw it on a spacetime diagram and it is a line (a plane, in full 3D). Change your speed and the line tilts — sweeping over events that were, a moment ago, in your future.
light cone
The boundary of everything you could possibly influence or be influenced by, drawn at 45° on these diagrams. Inside it, cause and effect; outside it, no signal can pass. The cone is the same for every observer — that is what keeps physics sane.
timelike vs spacelike
Two events are timelike separated if a signal could travel between them — then every observer agrees which came first. They are spacelike separated if no signal could — and then observers disagree about the order, with no fact of the matter. The sim tests every pair and tells you which it is.
block universe (eternalism)
The view that past, present and future are all equally real — that spacetime is a fixed four-dimensional block and "now" is just where you happen to be in it, like "here". The rival view, presentism, says only the present moment exists.
A-series and B-series
McTaggart's distinction. The A-series describes events as past, present or future — which changes as time passes. The B-series describes them only as earlier-than or later-than — which never changes. Relativity is comfortable with the B-series and awkward with the A.
$$ t' = \gamma\left(t - \frac{vx}{c^2}\right) \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \qquad \Delta t = \frac{\gamma v x}{c^2} $$

The last expression is the whole trick: a tiny v multiplied by an enormous x gives a large Δt. Walking pace (1 m/s) and the distance to Andromeda (2.4×10²² m) give 2.67×10⁵ s — about three days.

Start with something that is not in dispute. The speed of light is the same for every observer, however fast they are moving — measured, repeatedly, since 1887. Einstein took that fact seriously and Minkowski drew the consequence: space and time are one four-dimensional structure, and different observers slice it differently.

The first panel is that slicing. Your "now" is the flat cyan line. Someone moving relative to you has a tilted pink one. Drag the speed and watch it scissor. Events that you say happen at the same moment, they say happen at different moments — and if you push far enough, the pink and orange events actually swap order. One observer says the flare came first; the other says the rock cracked first. Neither is making a mistake.

Before that sounds like anything goes, notice the guardrail the sim enforces. The two events A and B — the cup leaving your hand and the cup hitting the floor — never swap, at any speed, for anybody. They are timelike separated: a signal could travel from one to the other, so one could have caused the other. Relativity permits observers to disagree about simultaneity. It absolutely forbids them to disagree about cause and effect. Turn on the light cone and you can see the boundary between the two regimes.

Now the second panel, which is where the size of the effect stops being abstract. Two people pass each other on a pavement; one is walking at a metre per second. That is one part in three hundred million of the speed of light — utterly negligible. But Δt = γvx/c² multiplies that negligible fraction by the distance to the Andromeda galaxy, and out comes about three days. Events in the walker's "now" over there are three days in the stander's past or future. Get in a car and it is nearly three months. This is Roger Penrose's version of the point, and the arithmetic is not controversial.

Here is where the physics ends. Everything above is measured or follows from measurement. What comes next is argument, and it is labelled as argument.

Rietdijk (1966) and Putnam (1967) reasoned like this: whatever is on my plane of "now" is real. Whatever is on yours is real. We disagree about which events those are. Therefore all of them must be real — including events that are, for me, still in the future. The future exists already; we simply have not got there. That is the block universe, and physicists tend to find it congenial because relativity treats time so much like space.

Howard Stein replied in 1968, and his objection is the reason this section is not simply titled "physics proves the block universe". The argument assumes that "is real for me" applies to a whole plane of simultaneity. But relativity never licenses that. What it gives you is a point and its past light cone — the events that could have sent you a signal. Define "real for me" that way instead, as Stein showed you consistently can, and the argument produces nothing. The relativity is untouched; the metaphysical conclusion evaporates.

So the honest position, and the one this page will keep taking: the tilting is real, the three days are real, and what they mean is not settled. Anyone who tells you relativity has proved that the future already exists is skipping the 1968 paper. Anyone who tells you relativity is irrelevant to the question is skipping the diagram. The interesting work is in between, and it is still going on.
