1.The anatomy of learning

Give the stick figure something to do, then zoom in: whole brain → neuron → synapse → DNA. Every level shows what actually changes when you learn — and it changes as you practise.

measured — neurons, action potentials, synaptic strengthening (LTP), the memory role of the hippocampus, dopamine reward-prediction signals, and gene expression for long-term memory are all directly measured, some Nobel-winning. The "brain as a prediction machine" framing (how it judges from priors) is a well-supported theory, labelled as such in the readout.

doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
neuron
The brain's basic cell — you have about 86 billion of them. Each gathers signals on its branching dendrites, and if the sum is strong enough it fires an electrical spike down its axon to other neurons. Ramón y Cajal proved in the 1890s that these are separate cells, not one continuous web.
action potential (the spike)
A ~1 ms electrical pulse: the neuron's voltage flips from about −70 mV to +40 mV and races down the axon. Hodgkin and Huxley worked out the exact ion currents in 1952 (on a giant squid axon) — Nobel Prize.
synapse & neurotransmitter
The tiny gap (~20 nm) between two neurons. The spike can't jump it, so the sending side squirts chemical neurotransmitter across; the receiving side has receptors that catch it. You have on the order of 100 trillion synapses — this is where nearly all learning is stored.
plasticity & LTP
Synapses get stronger or weaker with use — that's plasticity. When two neurons fire together repeatedly, their synapse strengthens (Long-Term Potentiation, discovered 1973). Hebb's slogan: "cells that fire together, wire together."
myelin
A fatty insulation wrapped around axons that lets spikes jump between gaps, speeding them from ~1 m/s up to ~120 m/s. Practice literally thickens the myelin on the circuits you use — measured in the brains of pianists.
dopamine (reward prediction error)
A chemical the brain releases not for pleasure itself, but to signal surprise — "that went better than expected, do more of that." As you learn, the surprise shrinks to zero. This exact signal is also the heart of how AI learns by reinforcement (see the AI page).
DNA & gene expression
DNA is not where memories are written. It plays two other roles: it is the blueprint that builds your baseline brain wiring before you learn anything (innate priors, reflexes, instincts), AND, when a synapse is strongly and repeatedly used, it triggers gene expression — the cell reads the relevant genes and builds new proteins that make the change permanent. That is how a fleeting short-term memory becomes a lasting one (Kandel, Nobel Prize 2000).
hippocampus, amygdala, cortex, cerebellum
Brain regions with jobs: the hippocampus files new facts and events; the amygdala tags things with emotion (especially fear); the prefrontal cortex weighs options and decides; the cerebellum fine-tunes movement and timing. Learning recruits different ones depending on what you're learning.
$$ \text{Hebb: } \Delta w_{ij} \;\propto\; x_i\,x_j \qquad\qquad \text{Dopamine: } \delta \;=\; r - \hat{r} $$

Left: a synapse's strength w grows when both neurons (x_i, x_j) are active together. Right: the dopamine burst equals actual reward r minus the reward you expected — it fades to zero once you've learned. The same two ideas power machine learning.

Learning is not the brain "filling up" — it is the brain physically rewiring itself. Pick an activity for the stick figure and follow the signal. Touch a hot stove and the hand pulls back before you even feel it: that's a spinal reflex, no brain required (Sherrington mapped these in 1906) — while a slower signal climbs to the brain to register pain and, crucially, to remember so you're wary next time.

Zoom in and you see where "next time" lives. A memory is a set of synapses that got stronger. Fire two connected neurons together enough and their synapse potentiates (LTP) — Hebb predicted it in 1949, Bliss and Lømo measured it in 1973. Practise a motor skill and, on top of that, the axons carrying it get wrapped in more myelin, so the whole circuit runs faster and smoother. That's why the tenth free-throw feels different from the first.

So how does the brain judge, and on what? Every decision is a prediction built from four ingredients: what your senses report right now, the priors stored as synaptic weights from everything you've learned, the innate priors your DNA built in before birth, and your current bodily state (hunger, fear, fatigue). The prefrontal cortex weighs them; dopamine scores whether the outcome beat the prediction; and the difference updates the weights for next time. The brain is, literally, a prediction machine that never stops editing itself — and DNA is both the original blueprint and the print shop that makes the important edits permanent.
