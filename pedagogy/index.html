1.The forgetting curve & spaced repetition

Watch a memory fade — then fight back. Compare cramming against spacing, or build your own review schedule and see what survives to exam day.

measured — forgetting was first measured by Ebbinghaus on himself in 1885, and the spacing effect (spread-out reviews beat massed cramming) is one of the most reliably reproduced findings in all of psychology. Caveat: the exact curve shape (exponential vs power-law) and the day-numbers depend on the material and the person — the model here is a clean, honest approximation, not a promise for your specific exam.

the papers behind this simulation — search these in your library: 1885 Ebbinghaus - Über das Gedächtnis: Untersuchungen zur experimentellen Psychologie (Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology) 1994 Bjork - Memory and Metamemory Considerations in the Training of Human Beings 1994 Woźniak, Gorzelańczyk - Optimization of Repetition Spacing in the Practice of Learning 2006 Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, Rohrer - Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
the forgetting curve
How fast a memory decays once you stop reviewing it. Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and re-tested himself over days: retention drops steeply at first, then levels off. Roughly R = e−t/S — an exponential fall.
memory strength / stability (S)
How many days it takes for a memory to fade appreciably — the "S" in the curve. A fresh, hard fact has a small S (forgotten in a day); a well-practised one has a huge S (survives for years). Every successful review multiplies S, so the curve gets flatter.
the spacing effect
The same number of reviews spread out over time produces far stronger memories than the same reviews crammed together. Reviewing something just as you're about to forget it is where the growth happens — cramming, when you still remember it perfectly, barely helps.
spaced repetition
The practical system built on the spacing effect (Anki, SuperMemo): review each item at expanding intervals — 1 day, 3 days, a week, a month — timed to catch you right at the edge of forgetting. Woźniak turned it into an algorithm in the 1980s–90s.
desirable difficulty
Robert Bjork's insight: making retrieval a little bit hard (waiting until you almost forgot) feels worse but learns better. Easy, fluent review feels productive and mostly isn't — the brain only strengthens what it has to work for.
$$ R(t) = e^{-t/S} \qquad\qquad S_{\text{new}} = S \cdot g(R_{\text{review}}) $$

Retention decays exponentially with memory strength S. Each review multiplies S by a gain g that is largest when you review late (low R) — the mathematical heart of why spacing beats cramming.

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus did something a little mad: he memorised thousands of meaningless syllables (WID, ZOF, …) and tested his own recall over hours, days and weeks, inventing experimental memory science in the process. His result is the forgetting curve: you lose memories fast at first, then slower — and without review, most of what you "learned" today is gone within a week.

But every time you successfully pull a memory back, it comes back stronger — the curve resets to the top and then decays more slowly. Here's the twist the experiments revealed, again and again: when you review matters more than how often. Cram five reviews into one evening and each adds almost nothing, because you still remembered perfectly — there was nothing to strengthen. Space those same five reviews out so each one catches you just as the memory is slipping, and the strength compounds until the curve is nearly flat. That is spaced repetition.

Try it: hit compare and watch "study once" collapse, "cram" do only slightly better, and "spaced" flatten out and survive to exam day — same number of reviews for all three. Then switch to build your own, click the timeline to place your reviews, and find a schedule that keeps you above passing on exam day. The lesson Ebbinghaus paid for so you don't have to: don't study harder the night before — study a little, spaced out, starting now.
