Doodle Semantics

Change one word. Keep everything else identical — the same film, the same statistics, the same six adjectives, the same shade of blue. Then measure what moved. Four experiments, drawn by hand, each one you can actually poke.

“The world is presented in a kaleidoscopic flux of impressions which has to be organized by our minds — and this means largely by the linguistic systems in our minds.” * Benjamin Lee Whorf, 1940 — the strong version of this claim did not survive testing. What did survive is on this page.

1.One verb, two memories

Forty-five students watched the same crash films. Then they were asked how fast the cars were going — and the question contained one of five verbs. Pick the verb and watch the estimate move. Then press one week later.

measured — speed estimates and the broken-glass follow-up are reported numbers from a controlled experiment with random assignment. The verb is the only thing that differed between groups; everyone saw the same film. The effect has been replicated many times and is standard textbook material on eyewitness memory.

the sources behind this simulation — search these in your library: 1974 Loftus, Palmer - Reconstruction of Automobile Destruction: An Example of the Interaction Between Language and Memory 1994 Loftus - The Myth of Repressed Memory 2020 Spielman - Psychology 2e (p.279)
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
leading question
A question whose wording carries an assumption. “How fast were they going when they smashed?” smuggles in a severe crash; “when they contacted?” smuggles in a gentle one. The witness answers the question they were given, not the one they were meant to be asked.
misinformation effect
New information that arrives after an event gets folded into the memory of the event. A week later the witness cannot tell which part came from the film and which part came from the question. This is why the broken glass matters: there was none in the film.
reconstruction (vs playback)
Memory is not a recording you replay. It is rebuilt each time from fragments plus expectations. That is exactly why a single verb can get inside it.
random assignment
Who got which verb was decided by chance, not by the participants. That is what licenses the causal claim: the groups differed in the verb and nothing else, so the verb is what moved the answers.

Loftus and Palmer showed 45 students films of traffic accidents and then asked, in writing: “About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?” Nine students got smashed, nine got collided, nine bumped, nine hit, nine contacted. Mean estimates ran from 40.8 mph down to 31.8 mph. Same film. Same footage of the same speed. The verb moved the number by about nine miles an hour.

The second experiment is the one that should worry you. A new set of 150 people watched a crash; a third were asked the smashed question, a third the hit question, a third were asked nothing about speed at all. One week later everyone was asked: did you see any broken glass? There was no broken glass in the film. In the smashed group, 32% said yes — more than twice the 14% in the hit group, and well above the 12% of controls.

The word did not just bias what they said. A week later it had been absorbed into what they remembered. Nothing in the world had changed. One verb had.

Note on numbers: the mph means and percentages above are from the 1974 paper itself. Psychology 2e, the textbook in my library, reports only the qualitative result — “much higher speed”, “more than twice as likely” — so I have gone back to the original rather than invent figures.

2.Same numbers, different word

Two descriptions of exactly the same statistics. Not a rounding difference, not a different study — logically equivalent sentences. Flip the frame and watch the decision flip with it, including in trained physicians.

measured — framing reversals are among the most replicated findings in decision science, and the surgery study was run on physicians as well as students and patients. The two percentages shown for it are quoted from Kahneman. For the 600-lives problem Kahneman reports only “a substantial majority” and “a large majority”, so this page shows no percentages there.

the sources behind this simulation — search these in your library: 1981 Tversky, Kahneman - The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice 1982 McNeil, Pauker, Sox, Tversky - On the Elicitation of Preferences for Alternative Therapies 2011 Kahneman - Thinking, Fast and Slow (ch.34, Frames and Reality)
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
frame
The wording that presents a choice. Two frames are extensionally equivalent when they describe exactly the same states of the world — 90% survival is 10% mortality. A perfectly rational agent would be indifferent between them.
invariance
The rationality assumption that equivalent descriptions should produce equivalent preferences. Framing effects are famous precisely because they break it, reliably, in people who are otherwise competent.
gain frame / loss frame
Describing an outcome in terms of what is gained (lives saved, patients surviving) versus what is lost (lives lost, patients dying). People tend to avoid risk in the gain frame and seek risk in the loss frame — the same outcome, opposite appetite.
System 1
Kahneman's name for the fast, automatic reading that happens before you deliberate. You cannot choose not to hear “mortality” as bad news. Deliberation comes after the frame has already landed.

Physicians were given outcome statistics for lung-cancer treatment. Half read that “the one-month survival rate is 90%”. Half read that “there is 10% mortality in the first month”. These are the same fact. Surgery was chosen by 84% of physicians given the survival frame, and by 50% of those given the mortality frame. As Kahneman puts it: “Medical training is, evidently, no defense against the power of framing.”

The 600-lives problem does the same trick with lives. Programme A saves 200 people for certain; programme B has a one-third chance of saving all 600. Most people take the sure thing. Now rewrite it: programme A′ means 400 people die for certain; programme B′ has a one-third chance that nobody dies. Now most people take the gamble. A and A′ are the same programme. So are B and B′. Only the paint changed.

This is the cleanest possible demonstration that wording is not decoration. Two sentences that a logician would call identical produce two different populations of decision-makers — and the people making the decisions do not experience themselves as being moved by wording at all.

3.Same six words, different person

Alan and Ben are described with the identical six adjectives. The only difference is which one you hear first. Reveal them one at a time and watch the impression fork — then watch what happens to the word stubborn.

measured — the order effect itself is a measured, repeatedly replicated result (Asch 1946, and the standard demonstration in Kahneman ch.7). The impression meter in the drawing is an illustration of the direction, not a reported score — Asch reports the reversal and the reinterpretation, not a value per adjective.

the sources behind this simulation — search these in your library: 1946 Asch - Forming Impressions of Personality 2011 Kahneman - Thinking, Fast and Slow (ch.7, A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions)
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
primacy effect
What arrives first weighs more. Not because it is better evidence, but because it sets up the interpretation that everything afterwards is read through.
halo effect
One early positive trait spreads a glow over unrelated later traits. Its negative twin does the same in reverse. The later evidence is not ignored — it is recruited.
semantic change of meaning
Asch's own explanation, and the interesting one: the later words do not merely count for less, they mean something else. “Stubborn” in an intelligent person is principled; “stubborn” in an envious person is dangerous. Same string of letters, different content.
coherent story
The mind's default is to build the most coherent picture available from whatever arrived first, and then to defend it. Ambiguity is resolved in favour of the story already running.

Alan is intelligent — industrious — impulsive — critical — stubborn — envious. Ben is envious — stubborn — critical — impulsive — industrious — intelligent. Read them slowly. Most people find Alan considerably more likeable, and this is not subtle: it is the reliable result of the demonstration.

The naive explanation is that we stop paying attention. The better explanation is Asch's, and it is sharper: the early adjectives change what the later adjectives mean. In Kahneman's words, “The stubbornness of an intelligent person is seen as likely to be justified and may actually evoke respect, but intelligence in an envious and stubborn person makes him more dangerous.”

That is the part worth carrying: this is not a bias about attention or effort. The word arrived intact and was re-read. Which means the order in which you learn about a person — or a codebase, or a candidate, or a bug report — is not neutral packaging around the evidence. It is part of the evidence you end up with.

4.How the blue experiment actually works

This one is worth walking through slowly before you play with it, because the trick is easy to miss. Six steps: press next step and read the line under the drawing each time.

measured — steps 2 to 6 are the study as reported. The pink/red analogy in step 1 is mine, not part of the experiment, and is labelled as an analogy on the drawing. The response-time bars show direction only, not milliseconds.

the sources behind this simulation — search these in your library: 2007 Winawer, Witthoft, Frank, Wu, Wade, Boroditsky - Russian Blues Reveal Effects of Language on Color Discrimination 2020 Sedivy - Language in Mind: An Introduction to Psycholinguistics (ch.13)

The thing that is easy to miss is in step 4. The two trials use the same physical difference — two steps along the same ramp of blues, the same gap in wavelength, a gap you could measure with an instrument and get the same number twice. The only thing that changes between them is whether a Russian speaker would give the two squares the same name or two different names.

That is the whole design. If naming were irrelevant to seeing, both trials should take the same time. For English speakers they do. For Russian speakers they don't.

And step 5 is what turns a curiosity into evidence. The obvious objection is that Russian speakers might simply be better practised with blues, for reasons that have nothing to do with words. So the experimenters loaded the participants up with a second task while they worked. When that task was verbal — hold an eight-digit number in your head — the Russian advantage vanished. When it was an equally demanding non-verbal task — hold a spatial grid pattern — the advantage came straight back. Practice does not care whether your second task is made of words. Language does.

5.The boundary your language drew

Russian has no single basic word for “blue”. Lighter blues are голубой, darker blues are синий — two separate basic colour terms. Slide a pair of blues across that line and watch who gets faster. Then load the words up with a digit string and watch the advantage vanish.

measured — this is a speeded perceptual discrimination task with reaction times, an English-speaking control group, and an interference manipulation. The finding — Russian advantage at the category boundary, killed by verbal load, spared by spatial load — is measured. The response-time bars in the drawing are illustrative, since Sedivy reports the direction and the dissociation rather than per-condition milliseconds.

the sources behind this simulation — search these in your library: 2007 Winawer, Witthoft, Frank, Wu, Wade, Boroditsky - Russian Blues Reveal Effects of Language on Color Discrimination 2020 Sedivy - Language in Mind: An Introduction to Psycholinguistics
doodle dictionary — what do these words mean?
basic colour term
A short, common, non-derived colour word that a speaker applies freely — red, green, blue. English has one for the whole blue range; Russian has two. “Light blue” is a phrase in English, not a basic term, the way pink is a basic term and “light red” is not.
categorical perception
A physically even sequence being experienced as bumpy: differences that cross a category line look bigger than equal-sized differences inside a category. Long known for speech sounds; here, measured for colour.
verbal interference
Occupying the language system with an irrelevant verbal load — in this study, holding an eight-digit number in mind — while doing the main task. If an effect disappears under verbal load but survives an equally demanding non-verbal load, the language system was participating in the task, not just describing it afterwards.
spatial interference (the control)
An equally demanding memory load with no words in it — hold a spatial grid pattern instead of the digits. This is the condition that makes the result interesting: it rules out “the participants were just busy”.

Three squares: one on top, two below. Which of the bottom two matches the top one? Press as fast as you can. When two very similar squares fell on opposite sides of the голубой/синий line, Russian speakers answered faster than when both squares sat inside a single category. English speakers, whose language draws no line there, showed no such difference.

By itself that is suggestive but weak — maybe Russian speakers just have more practice with blue. The interference conditions are what make it strong. When subjects had to hold an eight-digit number in mind through the trial, the Russian advantage disappeared. When the extra load was a spatial grid pattern instead, it came back. Something specifically linguistic was being used, live, during a task where nobody was asked to name anything.

This is the narrow, defensible version of the Whorf idea. Not “your language determines what you can think”. Rather: a distinction your language forces you to make gets recruited as a shortcut, and shows up as milliseconds in a task that is not about words at all.

!What this does not prove

Four solid results are not a licence to claim everything. Here is the honest boundary of what is on this page.

partially supported — the general claim “language shapes thought” is true in the narrow, measured senses above and false in the strong sense Whorf originally proposed. Treat any single sentence summarising this literature with suspicion, including mine.

It does not show linguistic determinism. Whorf's strong claim — that you cannot think what your language does not encode — failed. Russian speakers see every shade an English speaker sees; English speakers can learn the голубой/синий distinction in an afternoon. What was measured is a speed advantage of tens of milliseconds, not a difference in what is perceivable.

It does not show that everything is framing. The framing studies work because the two descriptions are logically equivalent. When two descriptions are not equivalent — when one is more accurate — preferring the accurate one is not a bias. The lesson is about equivalent wordings, not about all wordings.

It does not show that memory is worthless. Loftus's own point is narrower and more useful: memory is reconstructive, so it is vulnerable at the moment of retrieval, and the wording of the question is one of the inputs to the reconstruction. That is an argument for careful interviewing, not for distrusting all witnesses.

It does not measure occupational or professional labels. Whether calling a kind of work “manual” changes how people evaluate the people doing it is a reasonable hypothesis, and the four studies above make it plausible — but plausible is not measured. As far as I can find, nobody has run that experiment. If you know of one, send it to me and I will add it here with a proper evidence label.
